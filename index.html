Optimal Interpolation

An Interactive Introduction to Optimal Interpolation and Gaussian Processes for Earth Sciences

This tutorial explores Gaussian Process regression in the context of spatial interpolation of geophysical fields.

Introduction

Understanding the distribution and variability of sea surface temperature (SST) is fundamental to climate science, weather prediction, and ocean ecology. Yet the choices we make when interpolating observations into gridded products have real consequences for SST , dissolved oxygen , and other ocean variables: decadal warming rates, for example, vary by nearly a factor of two across widely used SST datasets depending on the interpolation method.

This is where understanding spatial interpolation becomes essential. Given sparse, noisy observations of SST at discrete locations, we need methods that can estimate temperature at unobserved locations and quantify the uncertainty in those estimates, while understanding how methodological choices shape the results.

Gaussian Processes (GPs) are a powerful class of models that treat the unknown SST field as a continuous random function. They encode prior beliefs about smoothness and spatial correlation through covariance functions, then condition these beliefs on observed data to produce predictions with quantified uncertainty. The mathematical formulation for Gaussian Process Regression is equivalent to Optimal Interpolation (OI), a popular interpolation method in Earth sciences. The interactive visualizations below illustrate how each hyperparameter shapes the interpolation.

The Spatial Interpolation Problem

The goal is to reconstruct a field from sparse, noisy measurements. Each observation y_i is a measured value (e.g. SST) at location \mathbf{x}_i, where \mathbf{x} is a spatial coordinate (e.g. longitude, or latitude/longitude, or latitude/longitude/time). Given n such observations, we want to estimate the underlying field f(\mathbf{x}) everywhere, where y_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) + \varepsilon_i and \varepsilon_i is measurement noise. This requires accounting for spatial correlation, incomplete coverage, and uncertainty.

Consider first the 1D case: SST along a single latitude (6°N) across the Pacific, where \mathbf{x} is simply longitude. Satellite observations are available at some longitudes but missing at others due to cloud cover.

The same problem extends to 2D, where \mathbf{x} = (\text{lat}, \text{lon}). Traditional approaches might use simple nearest-neighbor or linear interpolation. But Gaussian Processes offer a more principled approach: they model f(\mathbf{x}) as a random function from which our observations are drawn (possibly with noise), allowing us to compute posterior distributions over temperature fields given the data we do have.

Mean and Covariance Functions

A Gaussian Process is defined by two functions: a mean function m(\mathbf{x}) and a covariance function (or kernel) k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}'). We write:

f(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}\bigl(m(\mathbf{x}),\, k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')\bigr) \tag{1}

The mean function is optional and captures the overall trend. For SST in the Pacific, this might be a simple north-south gradient (warmer near the equator) or a more complex pattern obtained from a model or climatology reflecting seasonal climate.

The covariance function encodes how strongly the field values at different locations are related. For SST, the covariance function should reflect physical intuition:

  • Nearby locations are highly correlated: k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') should be large when |\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'| is small
  • Distant locations are weakly correlated: k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') should decay to zero as distance increases
  • Self-covariance equals variance: k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}) = \sigma_f^2, the marginal variance of temperature

Covariance Functions and Hyperparameters

The covariance function is parametrized by a few interpretable hyperparameters. The most common choice is the Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, also called squared exponential:

k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2}{2\ell^2}\right) \tag{2}

This visualization makes physical sense: given the temperature at one ocean location, nearby points are highly correlated (high k) and can be confidently predicted. Distant points contribute less information. The red dashed line marks 2\ell, where the kernel has dropped to about 13% of its peak. Beyond this distance, correlation is weak.

In oceanography, SST correlation structure is governed by local mixing and heating (short range), ocean currents that transport water masses over 100s–1000s of km (medium range), and large-scale circulation patterns that set background temperature gradients (long range). A single lengthscale is a simplification, but it demonstrates the key principles.

This kernel decreases smoothly with distance r = |\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'|. Three hyperparameters control the kernel's behavior:

  • Signal variance \sigma_f^2: The overall amplitude of SST fluctuations. Large \sigma_f^2 means temperature varies dramatically across the ocean; small \sigma_f^2 means it's relatively uniform.
  • Lengthscale \ell: The characteristic distance over which correlation decays. Small \ell means nearby locations are quickly decorrelated; large \ell means correlation persists over long distances.
  • Noise variance \sigma_n^2: Measurement uncertainty and small-scale variability not captured by the model. Larger \sigma_n^2 means observations are trusted less.

Combining the RBF kernel (Eq. 2) with additive Gaussian noise \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2), the full covariance between any two observations y_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) + \varepsilon_i and y_j = f(\mathbf{x}_j) + \varepsilon_j is:

\operatorname{cov}(y_i, y_j) = \underset{\text{kernel } k(\mathbf{x}_i,\, \mathbf{x}_j)}{\sigma_f^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2}{2\ell^2}\right)} + \;\underset{\text{noise}}{\sigma_n^2 \,\delta_{ij}}

where \delta_{ij} = 1 if i = j and 0 otherwise. The noise term only appears on the diagonal: each observation has its own independent measurement error, but the underlying function is smooth.

Given n observations \mathbf{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)^\top at locations \mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n), let \mathbf{f} = (f(\mathbf{x}_1), \ldots, f(\mathbf{x}_n))^\top denote the true (unknown) field values at those locations. The Gaussian Process model is:

\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{f} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}) \tag{3}

Each observation is the true field value plus independent Gaussian noise. The covariance matrix \mathbf{K}, with entries K_{ij} = k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j), captures correlations between all pairs of observed locations, and \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I} accounts for measurement noise.

Lengthscale

The lengthscale \ell controls the spatial range of correlations in SST. A small lengthscale means the GP treats nearby observations as nearly independent, producing a rough, wiggly fit. A large lengthscale means distant observations still inform each other, and the fit becomes smooth.

Notice that with a very small lengthscale (<1°), the GP essentially memorizes each cluster of observations but knows nothing about the gaps, so uncertainty explodes between data clusters. With a very large lengthscale (>8°), distant observations constrain each other and the fit becomes a gentle curve, but it can no longer capture local features.

Signal Variance

The signal variance \sigma_f^2 controls the overall amplitude of SST fluctuations. It represents the expected variance of the temperature field itself, independent of measurement noise. Physically, this reflects how much temperature varies across the region of interest.

Drag the slider to see how the signal variance scales the uncertainty band and affects how tightly the GP posterior follows the observations:

When signal variance is too small, the posterior mean flattens out and cannot reach the observed values. When it's too large, the model becomes overly confident that big swings are possible, which inflates uncertainty far from data. The right value reflects the actual range of SST variation in the domain.

Noise Variance

The noise variance \sigma_n^2 represents measurement error and small-scale variability not captured by the model. Buoys have measurement error on the order of 0.1–0.5°C. Satellite SST products can have bias and error on the order of 0.5–2°C depending on location and atmospheric conditions.

When noise variance is too small, the model overfits to measurement artifacts. When it is too large, real structure in the data is smoothed away. In practice, noise variance can be set from instrument specifications or estimated from the data using the marginal likelihood (discussed next).

Marginal Likelihood and Model Optimization

How do we choose the hyperparameters \theta = (\sigma_f^2, \ell, \sigma_n^2)? One approach is to maximize the marginal likelihood, i.e. the probability of observing the data under our model, averaged over all possible functions consistent with the GP prior.

p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \int p(\mathbf{y}\mid f, \mathbf{X})\, p(f\mid\mathbf{X}, \boldsymbol{\theta})\, df \tag{5}

The marginal likelihood has an elegant form under the Gaussian Process:

\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y} - \tfrac{1}{2} \log |\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}| - \tfrac{n}{2} \log 2\pi \tag{6}

This has three terms:

  • Data fit term: -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y} penalizes poor fit to data
  • Complexity penalty: -\frac{1}{2} \log |\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}| penalizes overly complex functions (automatic Occam's razor)
  • Normalization: -\frac{n}{2} \log 2\pi ensures the Gaussian density integrates to one; it does not depend on the hyperparameters

This objective balances fitting the data with maintaining simplicity. There is no need for separate validation sets or hand-tuned regularization; the marginal likelihood inherently prefers smooth functions unless the data strongly demands roughness.

This optimization is often performed numerically using gradient-based methods (conjugate gradient, Adam, etc.). The optimization is typically fast because even with thousands of observations, the log determinant and inverse are computed efficiently using Cholesky decomposition.

Conclusion

Gaussian Processes provide a principled, flexible framework for spatial interpolation of sea surface temperature. By modeling SST as a random function and conditioning on observations, GPs deliver not just point estimates but full posterior distributions with quantified uncertainty.

The key hyperparameters (lengthscale, signal variance, and noise variance) have clear physical interpretations. Lengthscale reflects ocean dynamical scales; signal variance captures the expected range of temperature variation; noise variance accounts for measurement error. Interactive tuning and visualization of these parameters builds intuition.
