Optimal Interpolation

An Interactive Introduction to Optimal Interpolation and Gaussian Processes for Earth Sciences

Introduction

Understanding the distribution and variability of sea surface temperature (SST) across the Pacific Ocean is fundamental to climate science, weather prediction, and ocean ecology.

This is where spatial interpolation becomes essential. Given sparse, noisy observations of SST at discrete locations, we need methods that can estimate temperature at unobserved locations and quantify the uncertainty in those estimates.

Gaussian Processes (GPs) are a powerful class of models that treat the unknown SST field as a continuous random function. They encode prior beliefs about smoothness and spatial correlation through covariance functions, then condition these beliefs on observed data to produce predictions with quantified uncertainty. The mathematical formulation for Gaussian Process Regression is identical to Optimal Interpolation, a popular interpolation method in Earth sciences. This tutorial will walk you through the theory and practice of using GPs for interpolation, with interactive visualizations showing how each component affects the final predictions.

The Spatial Interpolation Problem

Imagine you are tasked with reconstructing the temperature field across the entire Pacific Ocean. You have temperature observations y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_n at n locations \mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n, where each location is a 2D coordinate (latitude, longitude). Your goal is to estimate the temperature function f(\mathbf{x}) everywhere across the ocean, accounting for:

  • Spatial correlation: Nearby locations tend to have similar temperatures due to ocean currents, heat transport, and mixing
  • Measurement noise: Instruments have error, and satellite measurements can be affected by clouds and calibration issues
  • Incomplete data: We have observations only at specific points; large regions have no data
  • Uncertainty quantification: We need not just point estimates but confidence intervals or full probability distributions

The same effect is visible in 2D. Below are raw satellite observations in a small subdomain (1–6°N, 133–128°W). About half the grid cells are missing due to cloud cover.

Traditional approaches might use simple nearest-neighbor or linear interpolation. But Gaussian Processes offer a more principled approach: they model f(\mathbf{x}) as a random function from which our observations are drawn (possibly with noise), allowing us to compute posterior distributions over temperature fields given the data we do have.

Mean and Covariance Functions

A Gaussian Process is completely defined by two functions: a mean function m(\mathbf{x}) and a covariance function (or kernel) k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}'). We write:

f(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}\bigl(m(\mathbf{x}),\, k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')\bigr) \tag{1}

The mean function captures the overall trend in SST. For the Pacific, this might be a simple north-south gradient (warmer near the equator) or a more complex pattern obtained from a model or climatology reflecting seasonal climate. The covariance function encodes how strongly temperatures at different locations are related.

For SST, the covariance function should reflect physical intuition:

  • Nearby locations are highly correlated: k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') should be large when |\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'| is small
  • Distant locations are weakly correlated: k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') should decay to zero as distance increases
  • Self-covariance equals variance: k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}) = \sigma_f^2, the marginal variance of temperature
Physical insight: SST correlation scales vary with ocean dynamics—decaying over 100–500 km in the tropics but persisting over 1000+ km in mid-latitudes where large-scale eddies and gyres dominate.

Covariance Functions and Hyperparameters

We parametrize our covariance function using a few interpretable hyperparameters. The most common choice is the Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, also called squared exponential:

k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2}{2\ell^2}\right) \tag{2}

This visualization makes physical sense: if you know the temperature at one ocean location, nearby points are highly correlated (high k) so you can confidently predict them. Distant points contribute less information. The red dashed line marks 2\ell, where the kernel has dropped to about 13% of its peak—beyond this distance, correlation is weak.

In oceanography, SST correlation structure is governed by local mixing and heating (short range), ocean currents that transport water masses over 100s–1000s of km (medium range), and large-scale circulation patterns that set background temperature gradients (long range). A single lengthscale is a simplification, but it demonstrates the key principles.

This kernel decreases smoothly with distance r = |\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'|. Three hyperparameters control the kernel's behavior:

  • Signal variance \sigma_f^2: The overall amplitude of SST fluctuations. Large \sigma_f^2 means temperature varies dramatically across the ocean; small \sigma_f^2 means it's relatively uniform.
  • Lengthscale \ell: The characteristic distance over which correlation decays. Small \ell means nearby locations are quickly decorrelated; large \ell means correlation persists over long distances.
  • Noise variance \sigma_n^2: Measurement uncertainty and small-scale variability we don't model. Larger \sigma_n^2 means we trust observations less.

Combining the RBF kernel (Eq. 2) with additive Gaussian noise \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2), the full covariance between any two observations y_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) + \varepsilon_i and y_j = f(\mathbf{x}_j) + \varepsilon_j is:

\operatorname{cov}(y_i, y_j) = \underbrace{\sigma_f^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2}{2\ell^2}\right)}_{\text{RBF kernel } k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)} + \;\underbrace{\sigma_n^2 \,\delta_{ij}}_{\text{noise}}

where \delta_{ij} = 1 if i = j and 0 otherwise. The noise term only appears on the diagonal—each observation has its own independent measurement error, but the underlying function is smooth.

Alternative kernels: The Matérn kernel is sometimes preferred for natural phenomena because it allows finer control over smoothness. SST fields are smooth but not infinitely smooth, making Matérn a natural choice in practice. For this tutorial, we focus on RBF for clarity.

Given observations \mathbf{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)^\top at locations \mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n), the Gaussian Process model is:

\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{f} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}) \tag{3}

The observations are modeled as the true function plus independent Gaussian noise. The covariance matrix \mathbf{K} captures correlations between all pairs of observed locations, and \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I} accounts for measurement noise.

The Lengthscale Parameter

The lengthscale \ell controls the spatial range of correlations in SST. A small lengthscale means the GP treats nearby observations as nearly independent—the fit is rough and wiggly. A large lengthscale means distant observations still inform each other—the fit becomes smooth.

Noise Variance and Measurement Uncertainty

The noise variance \sigma_n^2 represents measurement error and small-scale variability we don't model. Buoys have measurement error on the order of 0.1–0.5°C. Satellite SST products can have bias and error on the order of 0.5–2°C depending on location and atmospheric conditions.

Getting noise variance right is crucial for good predictions. Too small, and we overfit to measurement artifacts. Too large, and we ignore real data. In practice, we estimate this from the data using the marginal likelihood (discussed next) or from knowledge of instrument specifications.

Marginal Likelihood and Model Optimization

How do we choose the hyperparameters \theta = (\sigma_f^2, \ell, \sigma_n^2)? The most principled approach is to maximize the marginal likelihood—the probability of observing the data under our model, averaged over all possible functions consistent with the GP prior.

p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \int p(\mathbf{y}\mid f, \mathbf{X})\, p(f\mid\mathbf{X}, \boldsymbol{\theta})\, df \tag{5}

The marginal likelihood has an elegant form under the Gaussian Process:

\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y} - \tfrac{1}{2} \log |\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}| - \tfrac{n}{2} \log 2\pi \tag{6}

This has three terms:

  • Data fit term: -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y} — penalizes poor fit to data
  • Complexity penalty: -\frac{1}{2} \log |\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}| — penalizes overly complex functions (automatic Occam's razor)
  • Normalization: -\frac{n}{2} \log 2\pi — constant term

This objective balances fitting the data with maintaining simplicity. We don't need separate validation sets or hand-tuned regularization—the marginal likelihood inherently prefers smooth functions unless the data strongly demands roughness.

This optimization is often performed numerically using gradient-based methods (conjugate gradient, Adam, etc.). The optimization is typically fast because even with thousands of observations, the log determinant and inverse are computed efficiently using Cholesky decomposition.

However, marginal likelihood optimization can be unreliable when observations are sparse or clustered—the optimizer may fit the available data well but produce poor predictions elsewhere. In such cases, it is often better to set hyperparameters using domain expertise. For example, in oceanography the lengthscale is commonly prescribed based on the local Rossby radius of deformation (~10 km at high latitudes to ~200 km in the tropics), ensuring the interpolation respects known dynamical scales even when the data alone cannot constrain them.

Practical Considerations for SST Interpolation

Data Preprocessing

Before fitting a GP to real SST data, several preprocessing steps improve results:

  • Normalization: Center observations to mean zero and scale to unit variance. This improves numerical stability and makes hyperparameter values more interpretable (a signal variance of ~1 is typical).
  • Removing trends: If the data has a strong seasonal trend (warm in summer, cold in winter), remove it first. GPs work best on deviations from a mean trend rather than the raw signal.
  • Outlier detection: Real SST datasets can have bad observations due to instrument failure or calibration issues. Robust outlier detection (e.g., using Huber loss) prevents fitting to false spikes.
  • Spatial domain definition: Define clear boundaries for your region (e.g., Pacific Ocean west of 120°W, east of 120°E). Handle boundary effects carefully—GPs assume smoothness, but abrupt coastlines violate this.

Computational Considerations

GPs scale as O(n^3) in the number of observations due to matrix inversion. For large SST datasets (satellite data can have millions of points), this becomes prohibitive. Common solutions:

  • Sparse approximations: Use a subset of "inducing points" to approximate the full covariance. The Sparse Variational GP and FITC approximation reduce complexity to O(nm^2) where m \ll n.
  • Local GPs: Divide the domain into tiles and fit separate GPs to each. This is memory-efficient but can create discontinuities at tile boundaries.
  • Mini-batch training: Use stochastic gradient descent with subsets of data, updating hyperparameters iteratively rather than globally optimizing.

Handling Seasonal Variability

SST has strong seasonal cycles. The mean state differs between winter and summer, and variability patterns shift. Approaches include:

  • Separate models per season: Train independent GPs for January, April, July, October. This allows each season's dynamics to be captured fully.
  • Temporal GPs: Extend the GP to include time as a dimension, with a separable kernel k(\mathbf{x}, t; \mathbf{x}', t') = k_{\text{space}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') \cdot k_{\text{time}}(t, t'). This is powerful but computationally heavier.
  • Basis function expansion: Use a finite set of seasonal modes (e.g., first 5 EOFs of climatological SST variability) as a mean function for the GP.

Validation and Uncertainty Quantification

Always validate your GP model:

  • Cross-validation: Hold out 10–20% of observations, train the GP on the rest, and measure prediction error. The negative log likelihood of held-out data is a good metric.
  • Prediction intervals: The posterior variance provides uncertainty. Check that ~95% of held-out observations fall within the 95% confidence interval. If not, your uncertainty is miscalibrated.
  • Residual analysis: Plot residuals (true value minus prediction) against position, magnitude, and other features. Patterns indicate systematic errors in your model.
Real-world example: NOAA's Optimum Interpolation Sea Surface Temperature (OISST) is a daily, global 0.25° resolution dataset that combines observations from satellites, ships, buoys, and Argo floats using optimum interpolation—a technique closely related to Gaussian Process regression. OISST applies a bias adjustment for satellite data relative to in situ measurements, then uses spatial correlation functions to interpolate onto a regular grid with built-in uncertainty estimation.

Advanced Topics: Beyond Simple Kernels

Compositional Kernels

Real SST has structure at multiple scales simultaneously. A single RBF kernel is limited. More expressive models use sums and products of kernels:

  • Sum of kernels: k = k_1 + k_2 models phenomena at different scales. Example: k = \sigma_1^2 \exp(-r^2/2\ell_1^2) + \sigma_2^2 \exp(-r^2/2\ell_2^2) captures both local variability (short lengthscale) and regional patterns (long lengthscale).
  • Product of kernels: k = k_1 \cdot k_2 models interactions. For spatio-temporal data, k(\mathbf{x}, t; \mathbf{x}', t') = k_{\text{space}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') \cdot k_{\text{time}}(t, t') means correlation decays both with distance and time.
  • Spectral mixtures: A weighted sum of sinusoidal kernels can approximate complex spectra. This is powerful for capturing multi-scale SST variability.

Non-Stationary Kernels

SST properties vary regionally. The tropics have different dynamics than mid-latitudes. Non-stationary kernels allow lengthscale and variance to vary spatially:

k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2(\mathbf{x})\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2}{2\ell^2(\mathbf{x})}\right) \tag{7}

where \ell(\mathbf{x}) is a spatially-varying lengthscale. This is more complex to implement but captures regional differences in oceanographic dynamics.

Anisotropic Kernels

The isotropic RBF kernel assumes that correlation decays at the same rate in all directions. In the ocean, this is rarely the case: currents, coastlines, and Coriolis effects create preferred directions of correlation. An anisotropic kernel replaces the single lengthscale with a matrix:

k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2 \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}')^\top \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}')\right) \tag{8}

where \boldsymbol{\Lambda} is a positive-definite matrix whose eigenvectors define the principal directions of correlation and whose eigenvalues set the lengthscales along those directions. For SST, zonal (east–west) correlation lengths are typically much longer than meridional (north–south) ones, reflecting the dominant direction of ocean currents and atmospheric forcing. Anisotropic kernels can also be made spatially varying, so that the preferred direction rotates to follow local current geometry or coastline orientation.

Challenges and Limitations

Non-Stationarity in a Changing Climate

Both GP regression and classical OI assume that the covariance structure is fixed and known (or learnable from available data). In practice, ocean variability is non-stationary: the spatial correlation structure of SST changes with season, ENSO phase, and long-term climate trends. A lengthscale estimated from 1990s data may not describe 2020s variability well. Locally stationary approaches—fitting separate covariance parameters in moving windows of space and time —offer one path forward, but require enough data in each window to estimate parameters reliably. In a warming climate where the background state itself is shifting, static covariance assumptions become increasingly problematic, and methods that can adapt their covariance structure over time are essential.

Smoothing Over Important Gradients

GPs with isotropic kernels produce smooth interpolations by design. While this is usually desirable, it can be problematic near ocean fronts, eddies, and boundary currents where sharp temperature gradients carry dynamical significance. The posterior mean of a GP will inevitably smooth across a front if the kernel lengthscale is comparable to or larger than the frontal width, blurring a 2°C gradient over 50 km into a gentle slope. This is not just a cosmetic issue—smoothed gradients underestimate geostrophic currents and misrepresent the energetics of the flow. Potential remedies include using shorter lengthscales (at the cost of noisier fields away from fronts), barrier kernels that respect coastlines and known frontal positions, or physics-informed kernels such as the Helmholtz decomposition approach of Berlinghieri et al. , which encodes divergence and rotation structure directly into the GP prior.

Multi-Fidelity Methods

Real oceanographic datasets often combine observations from multiple sources with very different accuracies, resolutions, and spatial coverage: high-resolution but cloud-contaminated satellite SST, sparse but accurate in situ measurements from buoys and ships, and coarse-resolution model output that provides complete spatial coverage. Rather than treating all sources equally or discarding lower-quality data, multi-fidelity GP frameworks systematically exploit correlations between data sources of varying fidelity. Babaee et al. demonstrated this approach for SST in Massachusetts and Cape Cod Bays, using autoregressive GP schemes that combine satellite imagery with in situ measurements to produce predictions with calibrated uncertainty maps. Multi-fidelity methods are especially valuable in coastal regions where satellite coverage is poor and the interplay between different data sources matters most.

Conclusion

Gaussian Processes provide a principled, flexible framework for spatial interpolation of sea surface temperature. By modeling SST as a random function and conditioning on observations, GPs deliver not just point estimates but full posterior distributions with quantified uncertainty—essential for decision-making in climate and oceanography.

The key hyperparameters—lengthscale, signal variance, and noise variance—have clear physical interpretations. Lengthscale reflects ocean dynamical scales; signal variance captures the expected range of temperature variation; noise variance accounts for measurement error. Interactive tuning and visualization of these parameters builds intuition.

The marginal likelihood objective elegantly balances fitting observed data with maintaining simplicity, avoiding overfitting without need for external validation. For large datasets, sparse approximations make GPs tractable. Advanced extensions using composite kernels and spatially-varying parameters push GPs toward state-of-the-art climate modeling.

While we have demonstrated these methods using sea surface temperature, the same optimal interpolation and GP framework underpins gridded products across the ocean and climate sciences. Sea surface height from satellite altimeters, subsurface temperature and salinity from Argo floats, dissolved oxygen concentrations, ocean color and chlorophyll, and atmospheric reanalysis fields are all routinely produced using variants of the techniques described here. NOAA's OISST, the Roemmich–Gilson Argo climatology, and AVISO altimetry maps all rely on optimum interpolation at their core. The specific kernel, lengthscale, and noise characteristics differ by variable and observing system, but the mathematical machinery—covariance modeling, posterior inference, and hyperparameter optimization—remains the same.

Whether you're reconstructing historical SST for climate analysis, nowcasting temperature for fisheries management, or assimilating observations into weather models, Gaussian Processes remain one of the most elegant and effective tools in your toolbox. Comparison with Optimal Interpolation

For simplicity, we follow the notation from Ide et al. (1997). However, many different notations exist in the literature.

  • \mathbf{x}: true state vector
  • \mathbf{x}_b: background state (prior mean)
  • \mathbf{B}: background error covariance matrix
  • \mathbf{y}: observation vector
  • \mathbf{H}: observation operator
  • \mathbf{R}: observation error covariance
  • \mathbf{d} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}_b: innovation
  • \mathbf{x}_a: analysis (posterior mean)
  • \mathbf{P}_a: posterior error covariance

While notational conventions vary, nearly all OI literature uses \mathbf{x} for the state vector and \mathbf{y} for the observation vector—a sharp distinction from GPR, which we discuss in the comparison below.

Formulation

Optimal interpolation is a data assimilation method grounded in Bayesian inference. The goal is to estimate the most probable state \mathbf{x} of a physical system, given a prior (or background) estimate \mathbf{x}_b and a set of noisy observations \mathbf{y}. OI assumes Gaussian statistics for both the prior and the observation noise. Some consider OI a form of objective analysis—an objective, systematic approach to producing gridded datasets from sparse or irregular observations.

We assume the following statistical models:

\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_b,\; \mathbf{B}) \qquad\qquad \mathbf{y}\,|\,\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x},\; \mathbf{R})

Here \mathbf{B} is the prior (background) covariance matrix representing uncertainty in the background field, and \mathbf{R} is the observation error covariance. In practice, these matrices are set according to expert knowledge about the system, empirical correlations, or physical reasoning. The operator \mathbf{H} maps the state space to observation space.

Bayes' theorem yields the posterior (analysis) mean and covariance:

\mathbf{x}_a = \mathbf{x}_b + \mathbf{K}\bigl(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}_b\bigr) \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{H}^\top\bigl(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{H}^\top + \mathbf{R}\bigr)^{-1} \mathbf{P}_a = \bigl(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{H}\bigr)\mathbf{B}

The gain matrix \mathbf{K} determines how much weight is given to the innovation—the discrepancy between the observations and the background projected into observation space. It balances confidence in the observations (via \mathbf{R}) against confidence in the background (via \mathbf{B}). A larger \mathbf{R} results in more reliance on the background, while a smaller \mathbf{R} leads to more reliance on the observations.

OI produces a linear estimate that minimizes the expected mean squared error under Gaussian assumptions. Intuitively, the analysis \mathbf{x}_a is a weighted average between the prior estimate and the observations, where the weights depend on relative uncertainties. In regions with sparse observational coverage, the analysis is heavily dictated by the prescribed background state and its associated covariance \mathbf{B}. By definition, OI is only truly “optimal” when the exact statistical properties of the system are known. In practice, the background error covariance is frequently constructed using idealized formulations (such as scaling spatial decorrelation lengths to the local Rossby radius of deformation). When these idealized statistics differ too much from the truth, predictions in data-sparse areas become highly unreliable.

Variational Interpretation

OI can also be interpreted as the solution to a minimization problem with cost function:

J(\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x})^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1} (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}) + (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_b)^\top \mathbf{B}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_b)

The first term penalizes mismatch with observations weighted by observation uncertainty; the second penalizes deviation from the background weighted by background uncertainty. Minimizing J(\mathbf{x}) yields the analysis \mathbf{x}_a. This variational view is equivalent to the Bayesian formulation under Gaussian assumptions.

OI can be interpreted as a special case of the Kalman filter where there is no time evolution of the state, only an update step using new observations. It is also algebraically equivalent to 3D-Var (three-dimensional variational data assimilation) under linear and Gaussian assumptions . Because OI is strictly an interpolation method used when no forward dynamical model is assumed or computationally feasible, it cannot dynamically evolve the background covariance. Instead, the covariance must be prescribed using historical observations, empirical fitting, or expert domain knowledge.

GPR vs. OI at a Glance

Concept GPR OI
Prior mean m(\mathbf{X}) \mathbf{x}_b
Covariance k(\cdot, \cdot) \mathbf{B}
Covariance estimation Learned (marginal likelihood) Prescribed (domain knowledge)
Observation noise \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I} \mathbf{R}
Observation operator Implicit (identity) \mathbf{H}
Posterior mean \bar{\mathbf{f}}_* \mathbf{x}_a
Posterior covariance \mathrm{cov}(\mathbf{f}_*) \mathbf{P}_a

While used in different domains, GPR and OI are fundamentally similar: both provide Bayesian estimates from uncertain observations. GPR generalizes OI by allowing learned kernel structures and flexible prior means, whereas OI often leverages domain-specific knowledge for covariance specification—and prescribing the covariance manually is less computationally expensive than training.

One of the main sources of confusion when comparing GPR and OI is that the variables \mathbf{x} and \mathbf{y} refer to different quantities in each method, and this distinction should be noted carefully. In GPR the goal is regression, so the notation follows the usual machine-learning convention: \mathbf{x} represents the input variables (regressors) and \mathbf{y} represents the predicted quantity (regressand). In contrast, OI is a state-estimation method: \mathbf{x} denotes the state we want to estimate, and \mathbf{y} denotes the observations used to estimate that state.
